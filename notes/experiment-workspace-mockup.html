The short version

The question
Can a curriculum build one skill on top of another? You asked for three tests: stacking, combination, order.
What we measure
How far the jaws follow a cube moved 20 mm mid-episode (follow gain, 0 to 1), and how many pickups survive a soft elbow.
Outcome
Stack: yes. Combine: yes. Order: no effect. Read it as "skills add up, but these two do not depend on each other".

The setup. A policy is the network that drives the arm. Earlier we showed that one change to the training data gives the policy one skill. A teacher that corrects its own mistakes teaches it to follow the cube when it moves ("look"). Randomized servo physics teaches it to cope when the elbow goes soft ("motor"). A curriculum needs such skills to build on each other.

1. Do the two skills stack in one policy?

Why
If training on both changes gave neither skill, the changes interfere and no curriculum can work.
What we did
Trained one policy on half of each kind of data, then moved the cube 20 mm mid-episode, and separately made the elbow soft.

3. Does having one skill make the next cheaper?

Why
This is the curriculum question. If yes, the order you teach skills in saves data.
What we did

Can I trust it?

  • Same 100 placements and the same shift plans for every policy
  • The half-and-half policy has the same 7,200-placement budget as the three single policies
  • Both starts in the order test had the same training: 40,000 steps on 7,200 placements
  • The motor data and the look data were captured on different scenes
  • One training seed for parts 1 and 2. Two skills only. Simulation only.

What it means, and what next

Stacking and combining are supported. Order is not, but this pair was a weak test: neither skill needs the other. One lesson: the two "failed" marks only mean something because the bar was written down before the run.

A skill built on an earlier one: retry after a failed graspNeeds "look" first. This is a real test of order.

Research log entry draft

TL;DR: Two skills stack in one policy and work together in an episode it never trained on, but teaching one first does not make the other cheaper.

Problem. We knew one data change gives one skill. A curriculum needs skills that stack, combine, and build on each other.
How we know: the feature probes held 12 of 13 predictions, but each tested one skill alone.

Experiment. Train one policy on both data changes, hit it with both changes in one episode, and add each skill to a policy that does or does not already have the other.
What we hope to learn: if the start with a skill leads by 0.10, order matters. If not, skills are learned independently.

Outcome. Stack: yes. Combine: yes, at execute 30. Order: no.

Takeaway: This pair could not show order. The next test needs a skill that is built on an earlier one.
